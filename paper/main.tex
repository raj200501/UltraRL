\begin{abstract}
Reasoning systems must decide when to spend effort checking their own work. We study this decision in an exactly measurable testbed: an agent executes an arithmetic chain that may contain one silent fault, sees a noisy one-bit cue per step, and divides a fixed budget between executing, verifying and repairing steps. We train Double DQN agents with outcome-only and process rewards and several observation encoders, and test them on chains up to $2.3\times$ longer than in training (10 seeds, paired tests).
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Length generalization depends mainly on the observation encoder. With length-dependent features every reward falls below a never-check baseline on the longest chains; with length-invariant features the reward that pays most for informative checks keeps \val{main/ma_inv/heldout_long/success} success at lengths 7--9 and \val{main/ma_inv/heldout_xlong/success} at 10--14 at selected checkpoints, against \val{main/optimal/heldout_long/success} and \val{main/optimal/heldout_xlong/success} for an exact planner; at 10--14 a cue-triggered heuristic (\val{main/cue_triggered/heldout_xlong/success}) still beats it.
  \item Under every reward, agents drift into catching faults without repairing them. This is a learning failure under partial observability, not reward hacking: one observation bit recording that a caught fault is unrepaired removes it under all three rewards, and so does a window of the agent's last 3 actions; a window of 8 fails on long chains.
  \item Paying for informative checks makes checking policies appear sooner (median episode \val{main/ma_inv/first_above_half_median} against \val{main/outcome_inv/first_above_half_median} for outcome-only) and keeps them (\val{main/ma_inv/collapse_after_first_rate/pct} against \val{main/outcome_inv/collapse_after_first_rate/pct} of later checkpoints fall back to success $\le 0.35$). A step-correctness bonus, closer to a process reward model, helps less and never resolvably beats outcome-only at selected checkpoints.
\end{enumerate}
These results revise an earlier version of this study, which attributed the length-generalization failure to the reward.
\end{abstract}
