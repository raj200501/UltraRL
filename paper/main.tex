\title{Learning When to Verify Under a Budget:\\Process Rewards, Representations, and Length Generalization}

\author{Raj Kashikar}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Reasoning systems must decide not only how to solve a problem but also when to spend effort checking their own work. We study this decision in a small testbed where everything of interest is exactly measurable. An agent executes an arithmetic chain that may contain one silent fault, sees a noisy one-bit cue per step, and must divide a fixed budget between executing, verifying and repairing steps. We train small Double DQN agents with outcome-only, naive process and monitorability-aware rewards, using two encodings of the same observation, and evaluate them on chains up to $2.3\times$ longer than any seen in training (10 seeds, bootstrap CIs). Three findings emerge.
(1)~Process rewards make checking learnable: on training lengths the monitorability-aware reward reaches \val{main/ma_inv/train_like/success} success, versus \val{main/outcome_inv/train_like/success} for outcome-only reward, whose greedy policies have stopped verifying in \val{main/outcome_inv/collapse_rate/pct} of late checkpoints (\val{main/ma_inv/collapse_rate/pct} for the monitorability-aware reward).
(2)~Whether checking transfers to longer chains depends mainly on the observation encoding. With absolute position features, every learned agent falls \emph{below} a never-verify baseline on chains of length 10--14. With length-invariant features, the same reward keeps \val{main/ma_inv/heldout_long/success} success at lengths 7--9 (matching a hand-coded cue-triggered policy) and \val{main/ma_inv/heldout_xlong/success} at 10--14.
(3)~Throughout training, the agents' greedy policies switch abruptly between checking and not checking, so last-checkpoint evaluation substantially understates what runs learn.
These results revise an earlier version of this study, which attributed the length-generalization failure to the reward itself.
\end{abstract}
