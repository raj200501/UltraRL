\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Reinforcement learning has produced language models that spend test-time compute on long chains of reasoning, including checking and revising their own steps~\citep{openai2024reasoning,deepseek2025r1,kumar2024score}, and spending inference compute on search and revision can be worth more than scaling the model~\citep{snell2024scaling}. Two lines of work make this behaviour a target in its own right. Process supervision rewards individual reasoning steps rather than only final answers~\citep{uesato2022process,lightman2024verify}. Work on chain-of-thought monitorability asks whether reasoning traces stay legible enough to oversee, and warns that optimising against a monitor can make them less so~\citep{baker2025monitoring,korbak2025cot,openai2025monitorability}. Both lines touch a basic metareasoning question~\citep{russell1991principles,hay2012selecting}: with a finite budget, \emph{which} steps are worth checking, and does a learned answer to that question survive when the problem gets longer?

We borrow the term ``monitorability-aware'' from the original version of this study for a reward that pays for checks which keep a trajectory locally diagnosable. The testbed has no monitor and no notion of trace legibility, so this reward is a toy proxy, and our results bear on process supervision of \emph{checking} rather than on chain-of-thought monitoring.

That question is hard to study in language models, where the location of an error, the value of a check and the faithfulness of a trace~\citep{turpin2023unfaithful,lanham2023faithfulness,chen2025reasoning} are all hard to measure. We therefore isolate it in a small testbed (\S\ref{sec:setup}). An agent executes an arithmetic chain that may contain one silent fault. It sees a noisy one-bit cue per step and can execute, verify, undo or commit, each non-commit action consuming a fixed budget. The task admits strong hand-coded policies (a cue-triggered checker solves \val{main/cue_triggered/lengths/min/pct}--\val{main/cue_triggered/lengths/max/pct}\% of episodes in every length range we test, and an exact planner that knows the chain length and never goes back to check an earlier step \val{main/optimal/lengths/min/pct}--\val{main/optimal/lengths/max/pct}\%), so failures of learning cannot be blamed on an impossible task. Because every quantity is exact, we can separate three things that are entangled in realistic settings: whether an agent learns to check at all, whether it checks the \emph{right} steps, and whether the behaviour transfers to longer chains than it was trained on.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A small, fast and fully tested environment for budgeted verification, with exact metrics for faults, detection, repair and cue-conditional checking, several reward schemes and observation encoders, hand-coded reference policies, and an exact dynamic-programming planner that gives the best success achievable without going back to check earlier steps, which an exhaustive search shows is the unrestricted optimum on chains of up to 7 steps (\S\ref{sec:setup}--\ref{sec:method}).
  \item Evidence that length generalization is dominated by the observation encoder. Length-dependent features make every held-out observation novel and drive every reward below a never-check baseline on long chains; a length-invariant encoder recovers most of the performance. A control shows that removing only the position features is not enough (\S\ref{sec:length}).
  \item A failure mode shared by all rewards, where agents detect faults but do not repair them. We show that it is not reward hacking and that it is tied to partial observability: the observation hides whether a caught fault is still unrepaired, and one bit that records it removes the failure under every reward. A history of the agent's own last 3 actions gives the same benefit without the hand-made bit, and its last checkpoints generalize to long chains; a history of 8 actions, which also reveals position, fails on long chains, where its windows are unfamiliar and the agents spend slack they no longer have. The check payment's absolute advantage shrinks as the observation carries more, but outcome-only agents still fail at least \val{extremes/ratios/min/late_failure} times as often late in training (\S\ref{sec:norepair}, \S\ref{sec:followups}).
  \item Evidence that paying for informative checks helps agents find checking policies sooner and keep them. The naive process reward, which adds a step-correctness bonus and a token check payment, reduces how often agents' checking collapses; a substantial check payment reduces it further, under every learner setting we tried (\S\ref{sec:learnability}, \S\ref{sec:sensitivity}). Separated from the token payment and scaled up, the step-correctness bonus alone reduces collapse more the larger it is, also when paid only once per step so that redoing steps earns nothing; at its largest it prevents collapse about as well as the check payment, but its checkpoints plateau lower and no setting resolvably raises selected-checkpoint success over outcome-only. Ablations show that any check payment gives most of the benefit on training lengths; at selected checkpoints, removing only the cue-specific terms led to over-checking on long chains, an interaction with the term that rewards closing unchecked stretches that we did not isolate further (\S\ref{sec:ablations}).
  \item An evaluation under shifts in fault rate, verification cost, budget and cue quality (the agents degrade when checks cost more or the slack changes), a check of the encoder and reward comparisons under other learner settings, and an analysis of training instability (\S\ref{sec:shifts}--\ref{sec:stability}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{A correction.}
An earlier version of this project, using the same environment and rewards, concluded that monitorability-aware RL ``remains brittle under longer held-out chains''. A controlled re-run reversed that conclusion. The main cause was the length-dependent features of the original observation encoder, compounded by $4{\times}$ less training and last-checkpoint evaluation. The earlier pipeline also used a localization metric that gave credit for faults nobody checked, and it reused training seeds for one seed's evaluation episodes (harmless here, but a protocol error). Appendix~\ref{app:changes} lists every change. We report this because, at larger scale, the same confounds (length-dependent features and unstable policies) can make a reward look responsible for failures it did not cause.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Process supervision and verification.}
Step-level feedback improves the reliability of mathematical reasoning~\citep{uesato2022process,lightman2024verify}, and RL on verifiable tasks can elicit self-checking~\citep{deepseek2025r1,xie2025logicrl}. These works ask whether process feedback helps. We ask a narrower question in a controlled setting: when a process reward helps an agent learn to check, what has it learned, and does that transfer? Our process rewards are hand-specified with privileged access to step correctness, so they stand in for an \emph{accurate} process reward model. Process verifiers can also be trained automatically from rollouts~\citep{wang2024mathshepherd}, and rewarding a step's progress toward a correct answer, rather than its correctness, gives a better RL signal~\citep{setlur2025rewarding}. Our finding that paying at the repair step stabilises training (\S\ref{sec:followups}) is in the same spirit: what a process reward pays for, and when, matters.

\paragraph{Reward misspecification.}
Agents can score well on a specified reward while failing at the intended task~\citep{krakovna2020specification,skalse2022hacking,pan2022misspecification}, and process rewards used during RL for language-model reasoning can be exploited in this way~\citep{gao2024designing}, for instance by repeating steps that earn a step reward. Our largest step bonus has that loophole; paying it once per step closes it without changing the result (\S\ref{sec:learnability}). The detect-without-repair failure we find (\S\ref{sec:norepair}) looks superficially like such gaming. However, it scores \emph{worse} on the specified reward than the intended behaviour, so it is a failure of optimisation rather than of specification. It is loosely the reverse of a difficulty reported for language models, which struggle to correct their reasoning without external feedback~\citep{huang2024selfcorrect} and often fail to find their own errors but can correct them once the error is located~\citep{tyen2024llms}: our agents locate faults reliably and then fail to repair them, although repair here is a fixed two-action sequence rather than a reasoning problem.

\paragraph{Monitorability and faithfulness.}
Reasoning traces can be informative about model behaviour~\citep{baker2025monitoring,korbak2025cot}, but they are not guaranteed to be faithful~\citep{turpin2023unfaithful,lanham2023faithfulness,chen2025reasoning}, and optimisation pressure on traces can induce obfuscation~\citep{baker2025monitoring}. Our ``monitorability-aware'' reward is a toy instance of rewarding traces that remain locally diagnosable: it pays for checks that catch or rule out faults and penalises long unchecked stretches. Our environment has no notion of a model hiding its reasoning, so we make no claims about faithfulness.

\paragraph{Metareasoning and adaptive computation.}
Deciding which computation to perform next is classical metareasoning~\citep{russell1991principles,hay2012selecting}, and adaptive computation time learns how long to think~\citep{graves2016adaptive}. Learning which computations are worth performing has also been studied directly~\citep{callaway2018learning}. In RL terms, a check is a costly, informative action under partial observability, as in work on RL with observation costs~\citep{bellinger2021active,nam2021reinforcement}. Our agents learn cue-conditional checking from reward alone; they are not given, and with the invariant encoder cannot represent, the posterior over fault locations.

\paragraph{Generalization and evaluation in RL.}
Deep RL agents overfit their training distribution~\citep{cobbe2019quantifying,kirk2023survey}, and neural networks often fail to generalize to longer inputs than seen in training~\citep{anil2022length,deletang2023chomsky}. Our length-generalization result is a small, controlled instance of this: in our runs it tracks whether observation features change meaning with length or take values, or combinations of values, that training-length chains never produce (\S\ref{sec:discussion}). Value-based learning can converge to poor values when distinct states share an observation~\citep{whitehead1991learning,singh1994learning,mccallum1996reinforcement}, and recurrent Q-networks are the standard remedy~\citep{hausknecht2015deep}; such aliasing turns out to drive the detect-without-repair failure we find (\S\ref{sec:norepair}, \S\ref{sec:followups}). Methodologically we follow recommendations to report many seeds with interval estimates rather than point estimates~\citep{henderson2018matters,agarwal2021precipice}. We also document policy churn~\citep{schaul2022churn}: greedy policies that change abruptly between updates, which makes last-checkpoint evaluation unreliable.
