\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What rewarding checks buys.}
Paying for informative checks did not unlock behaviour that outcome reward can never reach: with a suitable observation, outcome-only agents also find checking policies. What it bought was retention. Outcome-only agents mostly abandoned checking later in training. Agents paid a token amount for checks (naive process reward) abandoned it less often but still collapsed in most late checkpoints, and agents paid substantially for checks never abandoned it. The contrast between outcome-only and substantial payment held under every learner setting we tried; the held-out benefit of the token payment did not. On training lengths, most of that benefit came from paying for checks at all, not from any specific term (\S\ref{sec:ablations}). What decided whether the learned checking worked on long chains was its \emph{allocation}. The gap term, meant to keep traces diagnosable, rewards any check that closes an unchecked stretch. Without the cue terms to counterbalance it, agents over-checked and ran out of budget on long chains. Step-level rewards shape not only whether an agent checks but how it spends a shared budget, and in this testbed that mattered more for generalization than whether checking was learned at all.

\paragraph{Where a process reward pays matters.}
Agents under every reward drifted into policies that catch faults and then fail to repair them. We argue that this is a learning failure, not specification gaming, because repairing is worth far more than any detection bonus. The learned values agree only weakly: right after a caught fault they rank repairing above continuing by a small fraction of the true margin, plausibly because continuing leads to observations that also occur on track. Moving the bonus from the detection step to the repair step widened that margin, largely removed the failure and brought the last checkpoint close to the selected one (\S\ref{sec:followups}). The test confounds removing the detection payment with adding a repair payment. Even so, it suggests a practical point for step-level rewards: when the state does not reveal that a correction is still pending, rewarding the step that completes the correction, rather than the step that notices the problem, can make training markedly more stable.

\paragraph{What rewarding checks does not buy.}
Process rewards did not fix length generalization. With the original encoder's length-dependent features, the best reward still fell below the never-check baseline on long chains, where every observation was novel to the policy. Removing only the two position features was not enough, because the encoder's other features (budget fraction, slack scaled by the total budget, gap normalised by a fixed constant) also change meaning with length. A feature that adds the missing remaining-length information did not help, and it produced observation combinations never seen in training. For process-supervised reasoning models, this suggests that ``learned to verify on training problems'' and ``will verify appropriately on longer problems'' are separate claims, to be evaluated separately. Length-dependent features are a plausible way for the second to fail while the first holds.

\paragraph{Evaluation lessons.}
Two problems in the earlier version of this study pushed its conclusion toward a false negative: a representation confound, and last-checkpoint evaluation of unstable policies. A third, a localization metric that credited unchecked faults, inflated diagnostic scores without changing the main comparison. None of the three was visible in the headline numbers. We recommend four practices for RL on reasoning-like tasks:
\begin{itemize}
  \item report learning curves and per-seed variability;
  \item select checkpoints only on in-distribution validation;
  \item measure how much of a held-out set is out of range for the policy's inputs;
  \item test whether out-of-distribution failures move when the representation changes but the reward does not.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The environment is deliberately minimal. It has at most one fault, checks are exact, the cue is given rather than learned, and the agent is a small MLP rather than a language model. The ``monitorability-aware'' reward is a proxy with no monitor behind it. Process rewards use privileged step correctness, standing in for an idealised process reward model. The terms that help on long chains, the cue terms, encode the same knowledge as the cue-triggered heuristic. Their benefit is therefore evidence that well-designed shaping helps, not that a generic step-correctness reward model would: the naive process reward, which is closer to one, was not reliably better than outcome-only on held-out lengths. The exact planner is optimal only among policies that never go back to check an earlier step. We study one learner (Double DQN) with a small sensitivity sweep; policy-gradient or recurrent agents might be more stable or allocate their budget differently. The encoders differ in feature scaling as well as in length dependence. Coverage explains the held-out differences, but not why the absolute encoders also learn worse on training lengths. With 10 seeds, differences of a few points are not resolved. Only a family of six headline comparisons, chosen after the results, is corrected for multiplicity; the remaining $p$-values should be read as exploratory. Finally, no encoder we tried closes the gap to the cue-triggered heuristic on the longest chains.

\section{Conclusion}
In a small budgeted-verification testbed, rewarding informative checks helped agents learn checking and keep it. Whether the learned checking carried over to longer chains depended mostly on the observation encoder, and on whether the reward kept checking frugal, more than on whether checking was learned at all. A failure shared by every reward, catching faults without repairing them, disappeared when the reward paid for the repair instead of the detection. Correcting a representation confound turned an apparent negative result about process-rewarded RL into a positive but qualified one. We hope the testbed is useful for probing how process supervision shapes \emph{when} learned reasoners check their work.
